A small percentage can be hard to care about until we give it a scale.

Suppose a fictional business handles 10,000 kg of food a week. Avoiding a loss equivalent to 2% of that throughput would mean 200 kg in a week, or 10,400 kg over 52 identical weeks.

## Be precise about the percentage

Here, 2% means **two percentage points of total throughput**. It does not mean a 2% relative reduction in an existing loss rate. Those are different calculations.

For example, reducing a 10% loss rate by 2% relative to that rate gives a new rate of 9.8%, not 8%.

The [Small losses add up experiment](/#experiments) uses the first interpretation and shows the multiplication directly. It assumes constant weekly volume and the same avoided-loss share throughout the year. It is a scenario to think with, not evidence that a particular intervention will achieve that result.

## Keep the global context separate

UNEP’s [Food Waste Index 2024 announcement](https://www.unep.org/news-and-stories/press-release/world-squanders-over-1-billion-meals-day-un-report) reports 1.05 billion tonnes of food waste in 2022 at retail, food-service and household levels, including inedible parts. That figure describes a different scope from losses during transport. We should not use it as though it were a cold-chain loss estimate.

## Start with a measurable question

For a real business, the next step would be to define its baseline, the material counted, and how any change would be measured. A useful early conversation might be:

> Where does loss occur, what evidence do we have, and which small change could we evaluate?

That question leaves room for sensors, better records, different handling decisions or AI — depending on the problem we actually find.
