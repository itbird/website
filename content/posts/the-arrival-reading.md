Two deliveries arrive with the same temperature reading. It is tempting to treat them as equivalent. But the reading answers a narrow question: what did this sensor record at this moment?

It does not reconstruct what happened before arrival.

## Try changing the journey

In [the hidden journey experiment](/#experiments), both fictional consignments finish at 4°C. One stays there throughout the trip. The other has an interruption at 16°C.

The chart deliberately makes the difference visible. Its degree-hour total adds temperature above the chosen baseline over time. That is a description of this constructed exposure, not a freshness score. The temperatures are teaching assumptions, not recommended settings for a particular food.

## What would we want to know next?

- Was this air temperature or a measurement of the product?
- Where was the sensor, and how often did it record?
- What food was being carried, and what had happened to it before this trip?
- Is the record complete, and can the reading be linked to the right shipment?

These questions turn a number into an investigation. They also make a useful starting point for an AI system: define the evidence before asking for an interpretation.

## Why the broader system matters

The [UNEP–FAO Sustainable Food Cold Chains report (2022)](https://www.unep.org/resources/report/sustainable-food-cold-chains-opportunities-challenges-and-way-forward) discusses refrigeration as part of food-loss prevention while also considering energy and emissions. Monitoring is one part of that larger system.

The practical question I want to explore is simple: **what needs to travel with a reading so that someone else can understand it?**
